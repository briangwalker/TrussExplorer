Table–balsa friction μ
Claw: a stick from the bottom-chord joint at each desk edge down the table edge to the underside of the table top, hooked under it. It can only pull the bridge down, so it carries load only when something tries to lift an end. It needs a joint at the desk edge, and the table top can't be thicker than the 50 mm allowed below the deck. Inside-edge brace: a joint pressed against the inside face of each table edge, held by a vertical stick from the desk-edge joint and a diagonal from the first joint inside the span. The face pushes it sideways, like the abutment of an arch. Friction gives some vertical hold: it holds rigidly up to μ × the face force; past that the joint slides and friction keeps pushing back with μ × the face force. Supports on the table top can only hold sideways by friction too (μ × the downward push). Measuring μ: tilt a piece of the table surface with a balsa block on it until the block starts to slide; μ = tan(tilt angle).
Support distance from the desk edge
0 mm = right at the desk edge; the right side mirrors it. With full-truss desk panels, the support point gets its own vertical and diagonal so the load can reach it. The pin sits at the left main joint. It only stops the model sliding sideways; under vertical loads its sideways force is zero. "Every desk joint can bear" adds rollers at the other bottom joints on the desks; any that would have to pull the bridge down are dropped, because the rules don't allow fastening it to the table.

    Member forces

    Sticks: use − and + to double up a member (its mirror twin changes with it). Area, stiffness, tension and crushing capacity grow with the stick count. Buckling grows n× for sticks glued in a row; at 4 sticks you can pick a 2×2 bundle instead (16× one stick). Tap a member in the drawing to find its row.

    One side truss (2D): set sticks here

    Forces are under the factored design load (FS already applied), so a member is fine while its use stays under 100%. Use = |F| ÷ the smaller of Euler Pcr and crushing capacity σcA in compression, or F ÷ σtA in tension. The course script only reports buckling; the crushing and tension checks are extra. The project rules ask you to hand-check member forces, so try a joint or two by hand before you trust this.

    Exact and instant: member forces and deflection scale with the load, so a new FS or book mass, deflection limit or overlap minimum needs no new solve.

    Low-fidelity pilot: these charts show only 1,000 random designs. Check the audit in the Sweep section, then run the full search.
    These charts show the last sweep, run before your latest input changes.

    Capacity is the book load at which the first member reaches its strength limit. Member forces scale in proportion to the load, so capacity = book mass × FS ÷ (highest use), where "highest use" is measured under the design load (books × FS). The safety factor is therefore already inside the number: a design passes the strength check when capacity ≥ books × FS (the orange line). Deflection is not part of capacity.

    Every stable design from the last sweep that also meets the envelope, edge and deflection checks. The line joins the designs that no other such design beats on both wood and capacity. Its slope is the wood it costs to buy more load. Hover a dot to see it; click to load it.

    Drag a box on the chart to zoom in. "Add designs around it" makes designs a few steps either side of the one you click (height, and any curve, overlap or support distance you are sweeping), in fractions of a mm. Pick a step or type your own.

    Every bridge you have had, newest first. It fills itself: a copy is saved before every reset, preset or file load, and about 15 seconds after you stop changing things. Pick a row as the base (the radio button) to compare the others with it: how much wood changed for each 1% change in capacity. "Load" takes you back to that bridge.

    Mouse as set in ☰ → Mouse and keys (left drag rotates, right drag pans, the wheel zooms by default); the cube snaps to views. Colour follows "Group by" above. Hover a point to see it; click to load it. Uses the designs from the last sweep or large search.

    How to read it: each line is one group of designs. For every load level along the bottom, the dot is the least wood any design in that group needs to still pass at that load. Flat stretches mean the same design keeps working; a jump means a heavier design is needed; a line that stops means nothing in that group can carry more. Pick the load you want to be safe at, then the lowest line there is the cheapest way to get it.
